# Editorial standard for public copy

This is the working standard for public-facing Oryx writing. It applies to new pages, substantive revisions, metadata, calls to action, notices, and translations. It is not a vocabulary blacklist and it does not override facts, legal disclosures, approved brand lines, or plain regulatory language.

## Write from the record

- Begin with the source material. Preserve names, dates, places, status, scope, and uncertainty exactly as confirmed.
- Make a specific claim only when the site can support it. If the evidence does not support a stronger claim, narrow the sentence or leave it out.
- Distinguish what is current from what is planned. Do not turn a direction, registration process, relationship, or enquiry into an offering, approval, guarantee, or outcome.
- Put the important fact in the sentence, not behind an abstract label. Prefer the named person, place, document, action, or decision.

## Make the prose sound written, not assembled

- Use direct sentences with a clear subject and verb. Let sentence length vary when the thought calls for it.
- Prefer one accurate point over a padded list of three. Do not manufacture balance with triads, paired contrasts, or repeated sentence frames.
- Replace vague praise and institutional filler with verifiable detail. “Useful”, “robust”, “world-class”, and similar terms must earn their place through evidence or go.
- Avoid template transitions, throat-clearing, and conclusion signals. Start where the substance starts.
- Use negatives only where they clarify a necessary boundary. Do not stack “no”, “not”, or disclaimer clauses as a substitute for explaining the positive fact.
- Keep intentional Oryx brand lines where approved. Do not flatten a distinctive, factual voice into generic corporate language.

## Structure and presentation

- Use sentence-case headings unless a name or official title requires capitals.
- Use bullets for genuinely separate items, not to make ordinary prose look organised. Avoid emoji bullets and pasted markdown residue.
- Use restrained bolding for a term that aids scanning, not for emphasis by accumulation.
- Keep calls to action concrete: say what the visitor can read, write, view, or do.
- Translate for meaning and local fluency. Afrikaans must read as Afrikaans, not as English reordered word by word.

## Editing pass

For each public-copy change:

1. Identify the factual source and the reader’s practical question.
2. Remove unsupported claims, broad promises, and decorative abstraction.
3. Read adjacent sentences aloud for repeated openings, mechanical rhythm, and unnecessary negation.
4. Check headings, labels, metadata, and calls to action as well as body text.
5. Preserve required disclosures and client-controlled actions exactly.
6. Review the English and Afrikaans versions independently, then verify that they make the same factual claims.

The goal is not to sound casual or to evade careful disclosure. The goal is precise, readable writing by a team that knows what it can say and says it plainly.
